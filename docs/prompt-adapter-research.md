# Ship the prompt adapter as a suggester

The evidence supports a prompt adapter for AntRouter only in a narrower, more cautious form than the earlier report proposed. Meaning-preserving changes to wording and format still move LLM outputs, but on current frontier models the average effect is a few points and is often not significant. The big swings of 60 to 77 points that get quoted come from worst cases on 2022–2023 models, and many of them were answer-parsing failures. No study has measured prompt transfer between commercial Claude, GPT and Gemini models. The cross-model losses that have been measured are largest when a prompt moves from a strong model to a weaker open one. They are small in the other direction, and AntRouter's targets are all peer frontier models. Trained per-prompt rewriters (BPO, PAS, FIPO) produce real gains, but those gains shrink on stronger targets and are partly inflated by judges that prefer longer answers. A plain "LLM, improve this prompt" step captured only about a fifth of BPO's gain. The best measurement of harm comes from rewriting real WildChat conversations. Even a GPT-4o rewriter fully kept the user's intent in only 74% of cases, added assumptions in most of them, and produced a worse answer about 8–19% of the time. Small rewriters did net harm when they also answered. The design the evidence supports has four parts. A few vendor-backed deterministic transforms are shown as a visible diff. Clarity and format suggestions are offered as optional chips rather than silent rewrites. An opt-in LLM pass rewrites only the user's own instruction, never pasted material, and lists the assumptions it made. And AntRouter's own evaluation needs to be larger and better controlled than the earlier report suggested.

## The earlier report overstated six figures and invented two rules

The earlier AntRouter report (`docs/prompt-adapter-research.md`) quoted several numbers that the new review checked against the full papers. The table lists what changed. Its rows are referred to throughout the rest of this report.

"Excludes→lacks" gave +62 on Flan-T5-XL and lowered Flan-T5-large. "21/25" is a Friedman test for significant differences. Rankings were actually flipped by some paraphrase pair on **15/25** tasks. The PromptBridge figures (99.39% → 68.70% vs 79.47%) and the BPO headline numbers (+22.0 on gpt-3.5-turbo, +10.1 on GPT-4) were confirmed exactly. The BPO numbers are differences in win rate as scored by a GPT-4 judge, not accuracy percentages ([arXiv 2512.01420](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.01420); [arXiv 2311.04155](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.04155)).

## Frontier models move a few points on average but ±30 on single questions

The robustness picture changed between 2023 and 2025. Hua et al. (EMNLP 2025) found that much of the sensitivity reported earlier was an artifact of rigid answer matching. For Gemma-2 on ARC-Challenge, the standard deviation of accuracy across templates fell from **0.28 with heuristic matching to 0.005 with an LLM judge**. Mean ranking agreement across prompts rose from ρ = 0.30 to 0.95 on ARC-C and from 0.15 to 0.90 on GPQA-Diamond ([arXiv 2509.01790](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01790)). Wharton's Prompting Science Reports reach the same conclusion from a different angle. Politeness, tipping and threats had **no aggregate effect** on GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, o4-mini or Gemini Flash. Removing the output-format instruction did hurt reliably (risk difference 0.086 for GPT-4o, p < 0.001). Wrapping a question in an "email" context hurt both Gemini Flash models ([arXiv 2503.04818](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.04818); [arXiv 2508.00614](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.00614)).

The catch is that averages near zero hide large swings on individual questions. The same reports found that one variant could move a single question by **+36 to −35 percentage points** ([arXiv 2508.00614](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.00614)). In ProSA, Claude-3.5-Sonnet and GPT-4o were *more* prompt-sensitive on HumanEval (scores 0.14 and 0.15) than Llama3-70B (0.07). Sensitivity was also higher on hard tasks such as MATH and Arena-Hard than on AlpacaEval-style requests ([arXiv 2410.12405](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12405)). For a rewriter, this means any given rewrite is about as likely to hurt a prompt as to help it, so rewrites have to be validated rather than assumed to be better. The most reliable lever is **output-format and structural specification**, not clever wording.

On transfer, PromptBridge shows that losses depend on direction. A GPT-5-optimized prompt lost 30.7 points on Llama-3.1-70B, but Llama's own optimal prompt lost only **2.4 points on GPT-5**. Between OpenAI models, o3 scored 92.27% on HumanEval with a directly transferred prompt and 97.15% after adaptation ([arXiv 2512.01420](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.01420)). No Claude or Gemini model was tested. The only cross-vendor data point is anecdotal: a materials-science extraction prompt refined on Claude 3.5 Sonnet "transferred effectively" to Claude 4.5 Sonnet, GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Flash ([arXiv 2512.22130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22130)). The earlier report described the adapter's job as "translating habits" between vendors. The evidence does not support that as its main job. Between peer frontier models, measured transfer losses are small or unmeasured. The value that can actually be shown is in **supplying missing specification**: format, constraints, goal, and keeping data apart from instructions. That is general prompt improvement with vendor-specific packaging, not model-specific adaptation.

## Trained rewriters gain 8–22 win-rate points, and naive ones gain far less

The papers closest to AntRouter's idea are trained per-prompt rewriters. Each one rewrites the user's prompt once and passes it to a frozen black-box model. BPO's gains shrink as the target gets stronger: **gpt-3.5-turbo +22.0, claude-instant +12.9, GPT-4 +10.1, claude-2 +8.8**, measured as differences in win rate under a GPT-4 judge. The ablation matters most for AntRouter: having gpt-3.5-turbo rewrite prompts with no preference training gave only **+4.6** ([arXiv 2311.04155](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.04155)). FIPO found the same pattern. Plain GPT-4 rewriting barely moved Llama3-70B on BBH (0.666 → 0.665), and an optimizer trained only with supervised fine-tuning made results worse ([arXiv 2402.11811](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.11811v4)). A zero-shot meta-prompt, which is what AntRouter would realistically ship, sits at the weak end of this range.

Part of the measured gain is length. PAS raised GPT-4-turbo's raw AlpacaEval 2.0 win rate from 46.1 to 65.3. On the length-controlled version the change was only 55.0 → 56.5, and on Arena-Hard it was 76.6 → 76.9. A weaker PAS model pushed Arena-Hard *down* to 73.5. The same paper found that BPO lowered scores on GPT-3.5-turbo-1106 and Qwen2-72B ([arXiv 2407.06027](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.06027v5)). This bias is well documented. Length-controlled AlpacaEval raised its Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from **0.94 to 0.98** by regressing out answer length ([arXiv 2404.04475](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.04475)), and MT-Bench lists verbosity as one of its core judge biases ([arXiv 2306.05685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). PAS also makes a structural point: it **appends** a complementary prompt and never edits the user's text, which avoids intent drift by construction.

The vendor tools offer little extra evidence. Anthropic's improver adds chain-of-thought sections, wraps examples in XML and adds prefill. Its claims rest on one +30% classification test and a 10-article length-adherence test ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/prompt-improver)). OpenAI's optimizer claims "generally a strict improvement" but warns it "may perform worse than the original on specific inputs". The dataset-backed version becomes read-only on Oct 31, 2026 and shuts down on Nov 30, 2026 ([OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-optimizer.md)). Google's zero-shot Vertex optimizer has a separate `gemini_nano` target mode, "since prompts that work well on large models may not work as well on them". That is vendor acknowledgment that the right rewrite depends on target size ([Vertex docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/learn/prompts/zero-shot-optimizer)). None of the three has been independently evaluated, and all three are aimed at developer templates, not consumer chat messages.

## One in four rewrites drifts, and small rewriters do net harm

The most relevant measurement of harm is Sarkar et al., who rewrote about 4,900 WildChat conversations in which users had signalled dissatisfaction. Two human raters scored intent preservation. With GPT-4o as the rewriter, **74% of rewrites strongly kept intent, 21% partly kept it, and 5% did not**. Most failures were in writing tasks, where the rewriter's own content moderation changed the user's goal. Rewriters added assumptions in **74 of 100** sampled conversations, and whether an assumption was plausible did not predict success. Win/loss/tie against the original was **68.6/8.0/23.4 for GPT-4o** but **23.8/40.5/35.8 for llama-3-8B** and 29.5/33.4/37.2 for Ministral-3B, each rewriting for itself. Small rewriters did better when GPT-4o wrote the answer, winning 45.5% and 52.4% ([arXiv 2503.16789](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.16789v2)). The rewriter sometimes followed instructions embedded in the prompt instead of rewriting them, and it could pass jailbreak attempts through in cleaner form. Two caveats apply: the intent raters were the paper's own authors, and the sample was filtered to conversations that had already gone badly.

Two further results argue against rewriting aggressively. Yang et al. (Findings of ACL 2026) found that models infer unstated requirements about **41.1%** of the time, and that underspecified prompts are about twice as likely to regress when the model or prompt changes. Yet specifying everything did not reliably help, and standard prompt optimizers added little ([arXiv 2505.13360](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13360)). CLAMBER showed that LLMs are poor at detecting ambiguity in the first place ([arXiv 2405.12063](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.12063v2)). People do tend to prefer a clarifying question when a query is genuinely ambiguous: 59% in open-domain QA, 63% in code search and 68% in a coding assistant ([arXiv 2305.13808](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.13808); [arXiv 2201.09974](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09974); [arXiv 2507.21285](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.21285)). On the HCI side, a CHI 2026 survey (N = 109) found that **79% wanted control and preferred structured options over free-form editing** ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3791726)). Dynamic Prompt Middleware found that task-specific controls increased users' sense of control, but users still found it hard to predict what a control would do to the output ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3729176.3729203)). No published study logs how often users accept, edit or undo prompt rewrites.

## Five deterministic rules have vendor backing, and vendors disagree on three others

All of the rule-based transforms the earlier report listed are plain text, so they survive handoff to the consumer apps. But the evidence for each one is mostly vendor guidance aimed at the API, not controlled studies. **Data first, question last** has the broadest support. Anthropic advises it for inputs of 20k+ tokens, and Google says "supply all the context first" ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices); [Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/prompting-strategies)). OpenAI's archived GPT-4.1 guide disagrees: it prefers instructions at both ends, or above the context if only one position is used ([OpenAI GPT-4.1](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt4-1_prompting_guide)). "Lost in the Middle" showed GPT-3.5 dropping from 75.8% to 53.8% when the answer sat in the middle of the context. In that study, repeating the query before and after the documents fixed key-value retrieval but had "minimal effect" on multi-document QA ([arXiv 2307.03172](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03172)). This supports putting the question last for Claude and Gemini and at both ends for ChatGPT, applied only when a long block has been pasted.

**Wrapping pasted content in delimiters** is recommended by all three vendors. Anthropic prefers XML. OpenAI says Markdown or XML and warns that "JSON performed particularly poorly". Google says to pick one format and use it consistently ([OpenAI GPT-4.1](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt4-1_prompting_guide); [Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/prompting-strategies)). **Toning down CAPS, "CRITICAL" and "MUST"** has Anthropic and OpenAI behind it, mostly based on overtriggering in agentic settings. Google's own templates still use "MUST" ([OpenAI GPT-5](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt-5/gpt-5_prompting_guide)). **Removing "think step by step"** fits the thinking models that most consumer modes now use. OpenAI says it "can sometimes hinder" results, and Anthropic says a request for visible `<thinking>` tags "may be declined" on its 5.x models ([OpenAI reasoning](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/reasoning-best-practices)). Controlled work agrees: chain-of-thought gave reasoning models "only marginal, if any, gains" ([arXiv 2506.07142](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07142)), and it cut accuracy by up to 36.3 points on intuition-type tasks ([arXiv 2410.21333](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21333)). **Wrapping examples the user already wrote** in `<example>` tags is safe. Generating examples is not.

The vendors split on three things: few-shot by default (Google always, OpenAI zero-shot first, Anthropic 3–5), rewriting negatives as positives (only Anthropic recommends it), and default verbosity (Claude Opus 5 runs long while Gemini 3 is terse). Politeness effects are inconsistent ([arXiv 2503.04818](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04818)). These belong in optional suggestions, not automatic rules. Two caveats apply to all of the above. No study has tested any of these transforms inside the consumer apps, where hidden system prompts and automatic thinking routing are in play. And the earlier report's "prefill returns 400 on 4.6+" note was not re-verified. Prefill is an API-only control in any case, so it does not belong in a handoff adapter.

## The design the evidence supports

The adapter should run after routing and look up a versioned per-target rule file, as the earlier report proposed. The default behavior is the main change. **Default to suggesting, not replacing.** The five supported deterministic transforms (placement, delimiters, CAPS normalization that leaves code and acronyms alone, CoT-phrase removal, example wrapping) appear as a diff that the user accepts with one click, with "send original" always available. Format, length, audience and negation fixes appear as **structured chips** the user can turn on, using vendor-aware defaults: a conciseness chip for Claude Opus 5, and a "more detail" chip for Gemini 3 only when the user's wording signals they want depth. This follows from the CHI 2026 preference for structured control and the ±30-point variation on single questions. **Skip entirely** for creative writing, prompts that ask to summarize, translate or edit pasted source text, and safety-sensitive content. These are the categories where BPO and Sarkar et al. recorded drift.

The opt-in LLM "Enhance" pass should follow the PAS and Sarkar lessons. It rewrites or appends to the **user's instruction only** and passes pasted documents through untouched, inside delimiters, as a guard against injection. It outputs a list of the assumptions it made, shown as editable chips. When a request looks underspecified, it asks clarifying questions instead of guessing. The number of questions (1 vs 3) is a parameter to test, not a known optimum. It enforces a length cap on the rewrite (about 2× the original, logged), because judges and users both reward longer answers. For a drift check, a cheap combination is embedding similarity, two-way NLI and a checklist confirming that constraints and entities were kept. This combination has not been validated for prompt rewriting. **Rewriter quality matters most.** The Sarkar data and RaR's result that a strong rephraser helps a weak answerer, but not the reverse ([arXiv 2311.04205](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.04205v2)), both argue against Gemini Nano as the main rewriter. Nano is reasonable only for deciding whether to offer a rewrite. The actual rewrite should use a capable model, with explicit consent whenever text leaves the browser.

**Evaluation needs to be larger than 30–50 prompts.** A two-sided sign test at α = 0.05 with 80% power needs about 85 non-tied pairs to detect a 65% vs 50% win rate, and about 200 to detect 60% vs 50%. With the 23–37% tie rates Sarkar observed, that means roughly **120–300 prompts per target** (a standard power calculation, not a sourced figure). The protocol should be pairwise judging with both orders evaluated, a judge from a different model family than the rewriter, length-controlled win rates, a human-labelled calibration subset with agreement reported, and several paraphrases of each seed prompt, as Mizrahi recommends ([arXiv 2401.00595](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00595)). In production, logging accept, edit, revert and edit distance adds a behavioral signal that has never been published for this kind of tool.

## Conclusion

The new evidence moves the adapter's rationale from "each model needs its own dialect" to "users leave things out, and some packaging conventions are vendor-specific." The main risk has also moved: a confident rewrite that quietly changes what the user asked for is now a bigger concern than losing a few points from not adapting. That makes the safest version of the feature also the cheapest and the easiest to defend. It is a visible, reversible set of vendor-backed transforms and suggestion chips, with an LLM rewrite held back as an opt-in that a strong model performs and that discloses its assumptions.

The most valuable thing AntRouter can produce is the measurement nobody has published: how much a prompt actually loses or gains when moved between current Claude, GPT and Gemini consumer apps, and how often users reject an adapter's rewrite. If the cross-vendor loss turns out to be near zero, as PromptBridge's peer-model asymmetry suggests, the adapter should be repositioned honestly as a clarity assistant rather than a model translator. That result would still be worth reporting.
